Approach

Find a real place first, then let physics draw it

The aim is to show the ocean as vast, strange and teeming with life, without painting it from imagination. Every new scene goes through the same process.

  1. Pick a real place and one phenomenon

    For example the coral reefs of the Paracel Islands, the octopus garden at the foot of Davidson Seamount, or the Arctic floe edge. The place needs published survey records, and the phenomenon has to explain one idea clearly: how light fades, how the tide sorts life into bands, how warm water shortens brooding.

  2. Read the sources and turn words into parameters

    Depth, temperature, water clarity, wave height and period, currents, sun angle, and the species, size, colour and density of the animals. The numbers come from papers, marine reserves, research institutes and encyclopaedias, all listed under Sources.

  3. Compute the picture with physical models

    One set of underwater optics, wave and ecology rules serves every scene: a new place is just new parameters. Where an animal appears is decided by its depth, temperature and habitat range, not placed by hand.

  4. Build the terrain and the animals

    Terrain, animal shapes and animation are all generated in code, with no external textures or model files, so the whole site is static and opens instantly.

  5. Check screenshots against photos

    Every scene is screenshotted in a browser and checked: do the colours look like real underwater photos, are the animals the right size, is anything unreadable or clipping through. If not, adjust and shoot again.

  6. Write the explanations, flag the simplifications

    Every feature has an explanation. Anything enlarged to be visible, simplified for speed, or estimated because no measurement was found is stated on the page.

  7. Narrate, test, publish

    The exhibition tour is voiced automatically on GitHub by MiniMax speech synthesis, in Chinese and English. Every push checks the build, that narration matches its audio, and that the docs are in sync, then publishes to GitHub Pages.

The ocean today

What changes every day with the real world

The repository downloads real-world ocean data twice a day. The water temperature and bleaching of three coral reefs and the tide at the Nanji Islands follow reality, and the Arctic and Antarctic show today’s sea-ice extent. The animals are still simulated; what changes is the conditions they live in. The table shows the latest download. The corals also have a “time machine” that goes back to any day since 1985: at the Paracels, only 3 of the 15 years from 1985 to 1999 reached bleaching Alert Level 1, against 14 of the 17 years since 2010.

Architecture

A static website plus two automated pipelines

3D scene

three.js r128 · WebGL

A hand-written shader patch, waterify(), gives every material underwater optics: separate red, green and blue attenuation, a scattering background, torch cones, caustics and sand ripples.

Source

src/ · {{N_SRC}} files

Split by feature (optics, seafloor, fish schools, each place, interface, tour…) and joined by tools/build.py into the js/seafloor.js the page loads. Only Python is needed.

Post-processing

render → bloom → white balance

Half-float rendering; bloom gives bioluminescence its glow; then white balance, vignette, torch halo and the heat shimmer above warm springs. Smooth mode turns bloom off.

Narration

narration/*.json → MiniMax

When the script changes, GitHub Actions calls MiniMax speech synthesis for both languages and commits the mp3 files. The key lives only in the repository’s encrypted secrets; the page just plays the finished audio.

Bilingual

src/00-i18n.js · i18n/en.json

Chinese is the source text. In English mode each string is looked up in a 1,000-plus entry dictionary; tools/i18n.py lists anything untranslated, and the checks fail if a string is missing.

Checks and publishing

check.py · smoke_test.py · GitHub Pages

Every push checks the build, syntax, narration-audio pairing and docs. Locally a headless browser runs the scenes, tour, map, postcards, VR and piloting in both languages. The repository root is served as the site.

Open issues

What isn’t done yet, or can’t be done in code

Each item says why, and what solving it would take. Those marked “needs you” can’t be finished by writing code alone.

needs you

Real ocean recordings

Sound is currently synthesised with Web Audio: surf, breathing and bubbles, sperm whale clicks, the hum of the deep. Real recordings (whale song, the crackle of a reef, seals under the ice) would add a lot, but recordings are copyrighted and can’t simply be downloaded.

recordings we are allowed to use (your own, or ones clearly licensed for reuse with attribution, such as some NOAA and MBARI public acoustic data). The editing, mixing by place and depth, and credits can then be done here.

needs devices

Testing on phones, gyroscopes and VR headsets

All tests run in a headless desktop browser. Smooth mode and automatic switching on stutter are in place, but performance on different phones, gyroscope orientation and WebXR headsets need trying on real hardware.

open the site on your phone (and a VR headset if you have one) and report anything that stutters or is confusing.

visuals

Animals are still simplified, code-built shapes

To run smoothly on phones without external files, animals are assembled from geometry, with no textures or skeletal animation, so they read as models. Octopuses and Sargassum look somewhat cartoonish.

for more realism, rigged glTF models: suitably licensed models (or commissioned ones), and accepting a heavier page.

data

Some values are estimates

The water depth at the Arctic floe edge is taken as about 300 m and the Nanji tidal range is simplified to 4 m, as no measurements were found; the global relief grid is about 37 km, so the trench shows only about 9,000 m deep on the map. All of this is noted on the page.

local measured data (tide tables, soundings) to replace the estimates.

models

The physics is simplified, not a numerical simulation

Schools use flocking rules, currents and waves use analytic formulas, and optics uses only three colour bands; there is no real fluid dynamics or radiative transfer. Fine for showing and teaching, not for research.

nothing for now; any single phenomenon can be made more precise on its own (for example measured spectra instead of three bands).

review

Content has not been reviewed by ocean scientists

All text was checked against published sources, but no specialist has gone through it line by line. The English is machine-assisted and hand-checked, and may read awkwardly in places.

for classroom or exhibition use, a marine biologist or physical oceanographer looking over the field guide and place texts; a native English reader flagging clumsy sentences.

accessibility

Not yet tried by an actual screen-reader user

The text description (G key) lists the current place, the readings and the animals you can see nearby, with each one’s direction from your line of sight and its distance; once the view has focus, the arrow keys turn it and +/− zoom, and the panels and tour work with a keyboard. All of this has only been checked by automated tests.

someone who uses a screen reader (VoiceOver, NVDA) trying it and telling us what reads badly or is missing.

What next

Where this could go

New places and phenomena

  • Great Barrier Reef bleaching · a timeline driven by water temperature, before and after
  • Sargasso Sea · a floating forest of seaweed in mid-ocean and its residents
  • Lost City vent field · white carbonate chimneys, set against black smokers
  • Humpback whale song · sound travelling along the deep sound channel, tied to the existing channel experiment
  • Submarine canyons and turbidity currents · sediment avalanching down a canyon
  • Dugongs of the northern South China Sea · historical records to explain “functional extinction”

Live data

  • Done · NOAA coral heat stress, the Nanji tide, Arctic and Antarctic sea ice: see “Live data”
  • Argo floats · measured temperature and salinity profiles driving the dive
  • Waves and sea temperature · from the same marine API, today’s swell for the kelp forest and water temperature everywhere
  • Local day and night · each place’s local time deciding day or night and the sun’s height

Experience and teaching

  • More languages · the language-pack structure is in place; a new one needs only a translation
  • Classroom mode · a teacher picks places and questions, students answer as they watch
  • Video export · record the tour straight to a video for sharing
  • Longer narration per place · from one clip to three: what to see, how it works, why it matters
  • A soundscape from real recordings · see open issues
